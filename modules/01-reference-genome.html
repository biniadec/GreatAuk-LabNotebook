Reference Genome

Module 00 covered how to work on Mjolnir. This is where the Great Auk project itself begins: finding, evaluating, and obtaining the genome we'll compare everything else against. You already know how to connect, navigate, and run commands — the questions in this module are biological and analytical, not mechanical: which genome(s) do we actually use, and what are the consequences of that choice?

This module works with genome assemblies — already-built genome sequences — because that's what you need in order to choose and download a reference. Later in the project, for comparing genetic diversity across species, we'll instead work with raw sequencing reads processed through one consistent pipeline. Published heterozygosity estimates from different papers are rarely directly comparable: they differ in mapper, filtering, genotype calling, depth, callable-site definitions, repeat masking, and more. Running everything through the same pipeline removes those methodological differences — though, as you'll see by the end of this module, it does not remove every source of bias, since the Great Auk itself has no living member of its own genus and must be compared against a related species.

Learning objectives

  • Determine which Alcidae species are relevant comparators for the Great Auk, and why
  • Search NCBI for available genome assemblies, and interpret GCA_/GCF_ accessions
  • Evaluate assembly quality using real metrics — not a single number — and justify a choice
  • Explain why the Great Auk needs a heterospecific reference, and what that costs downstream
  • Download a chosen assembly reproducibly, by accession, on Mjolnir
  • Organise a multi-species reference collection in a sensible project structure
  • Build a reproducible provenance manifest for the genomes you select
  • Explain how reference choice could bias diversity estimates — and preview how we'll test that directly

01

Who are the Great Auk's living relatives?

✓ Complete
Concept

Comparative genomics starts with knowing your comparison group. The Great Auk (Pinguinus impennis, extinct since the mid-19th century) belonged to family Alcidae — the auks — within the order Charadriiformes. Before picking any genome, you need to know which living species are close enough relatives to be scientifically meaningful comparators, not just "any bird genome." Taxonomic distance affects everything downstream: how useful a species is as a mapping reference, how informative it is phylogenetically, and how interpretable any comparison ends up being.

Pinguinus was a single-species genus — the Great Auk had no other living members of its own genus. Phylogenetic work places Alca torda (the razorbill) as its closest living relative, which is why the razorbill will matter a great deal later in this module.

Hint

Show model answer

For this project, the razorbill, murres, and puffins are the most immediately relevant — they're well-studied, more likely to have good-quality genome assemblies, and (for the razorbill especially) phylogenetically close.

02

Find available Alcidae genome assemblies

✓ Complete
Concept

Genome assemblies for most species live in NCBI Assembly (searchable via NCBI Datasets), with GenBank and RefSeq as the two underlying databases. An accession starting GCA_ is a GenBank assembly — submitted by any research group, not independently curated by NCBI. One starting GCF_ is a RefSeq assembly — a GenBank assembly that NCBI has additionally processed and annotated. A species can have several assemblies at once, from different groups, of very different quality — "a genome exists for this species" does not mean every assembly of it is equally usable. ENA (European Nucleotide Archive) mirrors much of the same data if you ever need an alternative source, but we'll centre this workflow on NCBI.

Task

Using NCBI Datasets (Genome), search for 2–3 species from your Exercise 1 list, including the razorbill. For each, record in your notes: how many assemblies exist, their accessions, and which (if any) is flagged as RefSeq.

Hint

The NCBI Datasets results table has sortable columns for assembly level and RefSeq category — use them to compare candidates at a glance rather than opening each record individually.

Show solution approach

There's no single command here — this is a database navigation task. A reasonable approach:

  1. Search NCBI Datasets (Genome) for each species name in turn.
  2. Note every assembly returned, its accession, and its assembly level.
  3. Check whether a RefSeq (GCF_) version exists alongside any GenBank (GCA_) ones.
  4. Do the same for the razorbill specifically — you'll need its best assembly for Exercise 3 onward.
Check yourself

For the razorbill, did you find more than one assembly? Independent of quality, what does the GCA_ vs GCF_ prefix tell you about an assembly's status — and what does it not tell you?

03

Which assembly should we use?

✓ Complete
Concept

Reference selection is a biological and analytical decision, not "pick the assembly with the biggest N50." Metrics worth weighing together include:

  • Assembly level — contig, scaffold, or chromosome-level (how far sequence has been ordered and joined)
  • Contig N50 / Scaffold N50 — the length at which half the assembly's total size is contained in pieces that size or longer; higher usually means more contiguous, but contig N50 and scaffold N50 measure different things
  • Number of contigs/scaffolds — fewer, larger pieces are generally easier to work with
  • BUSCO completeness — the proportion of expected single-copy genes found intact, a proxy for how much real biology is actually present and unbroken
  • Gaps — regions of unknown sequence (often shown as runs of N) within an otherwise joined assembly
  • RefSeq vs GenBank status, and provenance — who produced it, whether it's linked to a peer-reviewed paper, and how it was generated (short-read vs long-read, Hi-C scaffolded, etc.)
  • Primary vs alternate/haplotype-resolved — some modern assemblies split a diploid genome into two haplotypes rather than collapsing it into one representative sequence
Worked example (hypothetical — not real NCBI data)

Two fictional candidate assemblies for the same species, to illustrate that these metrics can pull in different directions:

Show reasoning

Neither candidate is simply "better." A is chromosome-level with a publication trail — valuable if you need chromosome-scale structure or synteny, and reassuring because it's been through peer review. B has far higher contig-level contiguity and completeness (likely long-read sequencing) — valuable for variant calling and heterozygosity estimation, where unbroken sequence matters more than chromosome assignment — but it isn't chromosome-anchored and hasn't been externally vetted yet. The right choice depends on what you're using the reference for, which is exactly why this is a judgement call rather than a lookup.

Task

Hint

NCBI Datasets' genome record page reports most of these metrics directly (assembly stats, BUSCO where available) — you shouldn't need to calculate any of them yourself.

04

The razorbill reference and heterospecific mapping

✓ Complete
Concept

The Great Auk has no living member of its own genus, so there is no conspecific reference genome to map its reads against. Instead, we use the razorbill — a closely related but distinct species. Mapping reads to a different species' genome is called heterospecific mapping, and it has real, predictable consequences:

Sequence divergence between Great Auk and razorbill
↓
More mismatches when aligning Great Auk reads to the razorbill reference
↓
Reduced mapping probability, especially in the most divergent regions
↓
Reads/alleles that happen to resemble the reference are retained preferentially
↓
Loss of callable genome, and under-detection of true heterozygosity

Ancient DNA compounds this: Great Auk reads will typically be short and chemically damaged (characteristic cytosine-to-thymine misincorporation near fragment ends), which makes divergent regions even harder to map correctly. We won't run any of this yet — the quantitative test comes once you reach the mapping module — but the causal logic above is worth understanding now, since it's the reason reference choice matters at all.

Task

In your own words, walk through what happens to a single Great Auk sequencing read that falls in a genomic region where the Great Auk and razorbill sequences differ substantially — follow it through each step of the chain above.

Hint

Think about what an aligner does when a read has too many mismatches to confidently place — does it map with low confidence, map incorrectly, or simply fail to map at all?

Check yourself

Why might heterospecific mapping specifically cause heterozygosity to be underestimated, rather than just noisier in both directions? Why does ancient DNA make this worse rather than just adding random noise?

05

Download a reference genome reproducibly

✓ Complete
Concept

A filename tells a future reader nothing, and a link copied from a browser can break, redirect, or silently point at a different assembly version later. An accession is a stable, citable identifier that anyone can resolve back to the exact same assembly, indefinitely. NCBI's own datasets command-line tool is built around exactly this: datasets download genome accession <ACCESSION> fetches the official data package for that accession, with no manual URL involved at all.

Mjolnir's software documentation doesn't list datasets as a pre-installed module (it names samtools as its example tool, not this one) — so, exactly as in Module 00, the first step is to check rather than assume.

Task

Check whether datasets is already available on Mjolnir. If it isn't, fetch NCBI's own static binary directly into your personal space (no admin rights needed) and use that instead. Either way, use it to download your chosen razorbill assembly by its accession from Exercise 3.

Hint

Two commands from Module 00 check whether a tool is available. If neither finds anything, NCBI publishes the tool itself as a plain downloadable binary — no module system or conda environment required.

Show command
# first, check whether it's already available module avail datasets which datasets # if neither finds it, fetch NCBI's own tool directly — a one-time setup step curl -o datasets 'https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pub/datasets/command-line/v2/linux-amd64/datasets' chmod +x datasets # then download by accession, exactly the same way either tool is obtained ./datasets download genome accession <ACCESSION>

This downloads a .zip data package (you'll open it in Exercise 6). The curl call here fetches NCBI's own versioned tool binary, not a per-species download link — the actual genome is still requested purely by accession, so the result is identical whichever route got you there. If datasets turns out to be available as a module after all, drop the curl/chmod lines and load it the way you learned in Module 00 instead.

Check yourself

Why is downloading by accession more reproducible than right-clicking "download" on a web page? Exactly what would someone else need — written down, nothing more — to redo this identical download in a year's time?

06

Inspect and organise the downloaded reference

✓ Complete
Concept

NCBI Datasets packages arrive as a .zip archive with a nested structure — typically ncbi_dataset/data/<ACCESSION>/ containing the genomic FASTA alongside metadata files (an assembly data report, sequence report, and similar). You'll need to unzip it, find the actual FASTA inside, and confirm it before trusting it — the same "inspect before you use it" habit from Module 00, just applied to a real, messier package this time. From here, each species gets its own named subdirectory inside your project's reference collection, so the structure stays legible as it grows: 

great_auk/
  reference_genomes/
    Alca_torda/
    Fratercula_arctica/
    Uria_aalge/
  metadata/
  analysis/
  raw_reads/

Species subfolders are named as they're added — you'll only have Alca_torda/ after this exercise; the others appear later as the Alcidae collection grows.

Task

Unzip the downloaded package, locate the genomic FASTA inside it, confirm it with the file tools you already know, then move it into a new Alca_torda/ subdirectory inside reference_genomes/. Also create metadata/, analysis/, and raw_reads/ as siblings of reference_genomes/ — you won't put anything in the last two yet, but the structure should exist.

Hint

unzip <file>.zip extracts an archive (new command — your first .gz files in Module 01 didn't need it, since gzip and zip are different formats). find . -name "*.fna" will locate the FASTA regardless of how deeply it's nested.

Show command
unzip ncbi_dataset.zip -d razorbill_download find razorbill_download -name '*.fna' file razorbill_download/ncbi_dataset/data/<ACCESSION>/*.fna grep -c '^>' razorbill_download/ncbi_dataset/data/<ACCESSION>/*.fna mkdir -p ../reference_genomes/Alca_torda ../metadata ../analysis ../raw_reads mv razorbill_download/ncbi_dataset/data/<ACCESSION>/*.fna ../reference_genomes/Alca_torda/

Paths are relative to wherever you downloaded into — adjust to match your own directory structure from here.

Check yourself

How many sequences did grep -c '^>' report, and does that roughly match the assembly level you recorded back in Exercise 2/3 — a small number close to a chromosome count, or a much larger number for a scaffold-level assembly? Separately: why keep each species in its own subfolder, rather than putting every FASTA directly inside reference_genomes/?

07

Build the Alcidae genome manifest

✓ Complete
Concept

A folder full of FASTA files means nothing on its own — without a record of what each file is, where it came from, and why it was chosen, neither you nor anyone else can reconstruct this work later. A plain-text, tab-separated manifest is simple, diffable, and easy to extend as you add more species to the Alcidae collection.

Task

Create metadata/genome_manifest.tsv with a header row and one row for the razorbill assembly you just downloaded. Include at minimum: species, common_name, assembly_accession, assembly_name, source, assembly_level, genome_size, contig_N50, scaffold_N50, download_date, intended_use (e.g. "Great Auk mapping reference"), and notes. You'll add a new row for each additional species as the Alcidae collection grows — the file just needs to exist and be correct for now.

Hint

Typing literal tab characters into a heredoc is error-prone. printf with explicit \t and \n is more reliable than echo for building a TSV line by line.

Show command
printf 'species\tcommon_name\tassembly_accession\tassembly_name\tsource\tassembly_level\tgenome_size\tcontig_N50\tscaffold_N50\tdownload_date\tintended_use\tnotes\n' > metadata/genome_manifest.tsv printf 'Alca torda\trazorbill\t<ACCESSION>\t<ASSEMBLY_NAME>\tNCBI\t<ASSEMBLY_LEVEL>\t<GENOME_SIZE>\t<CONTIG_N50>\t<SCAFFOLD_N50>\t<DATE>\tGreat Auk mapping reference\t<NOTES>\n' >> metadata/genome_manifest.tsv cat metadata/genome_manifest.tsv

Replace every <PLACEHOLDER> with the real values you recorded in Exercises 2–5.

Check yourself

Six months from now, could someone redownload exactly this file using only this manifest row — without searching NCBI's website again? If not, what's missing? Also: why record both assembly_accession and assembly_name, when the accession alone is the "real," permanent identifier?

08

Looking ahead: testing reference bias

✓ Complete
Concept

Exercise 4 argued, conceptually, that mapping to a related-but-different species' genome should cost you something. We're not going to take that on faith. Later, once you've learned mapping, you'll take the same raw sequencing dataset from one extant Alcidae species and map it twice:

  • (A) against that species' own reference genome
  • (B) against a related species' reference genome (the razorbill's situation, simulated on a species where you have the choice)

Then compare mapping rate, MAPQ distribution, properly-paired rate, depth, callable genome size, and heterozygosity between the two. The teaching point: same reads, same pipeline, different reference — any difference you see is reference bias, measured directly rather than assumed. Nothing about that experiment happens in this module; the reference you've chosen, downloaded, and documented here is what makes it possible later.

This also closes the loop on this module's opening point: even with a carefully chosen assembly, a heterospecific reference is still a heterospecific reference. That's precisely why the project will eventually move to raw reads processed through one shared pipeline for the diversity comparisons — it standardises the methodology, even though, as you now know, it can't fully standardise away the fact that the Great Auk's reference isn't its own species.

Task

Write a short prediction in your notes: for the own-reference vs. related-reference experiment above, which direction do you expect mapping rate and heterozygosity to move when using the non-native reference, and why? You're not running anything yet — this is a prediction worth revisiting once the real experiment happens.

Check yourself

What result from that future experiment would make you more confident in the Great Auk's razorbill-mapped heterozygosity estimate? What result would make you less confident?
